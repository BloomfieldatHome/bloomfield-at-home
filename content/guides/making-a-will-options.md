Without a valid will, the law decides who inherits, which may not match what someone wanted - unmarried partners, for example, can be left with nothing. Making a will is usually straightforward and inexpensive.

## Your main options

**Online will services**
Quick and low-cost, good for straightforward situations: a home, savings and clear wishes about who gets what. The better ones have their team check your will before it is finalised.

**Will writers**
Often visit you at home. Will writing is not a regulated legal activity, so check they belong to a professional body and have insurance, and be wary of high-pressure upselling.

**Solicitors**
The best choice for complicated situations: second marriages, children from different relationships, a business or farm, property abroad, or wanting to protect a home from care costs (be cautious of anyone promising this - the rules are strict).

**Free will schemes**
Some charities offer free wills, often hoping you will consider leaving them a gift (you are never obliged). Free Wills Month and similar schemes run at certain times of year.

## Making it legally valid

In England and Wales a will must be in writing, signed by the person in front of two witnesses who are both present at the same time, and the witnesses must sign too. A witness - or their husband, wife or civil partner - should not be someone who inherits under the will, or they can lose their gift.

## Common mistakes

- Not updating the will after marriage (which normally cancels an earlier will in England and Wales), divorce or a death in the family.
- Forgetting to name a backup executor or backup beneficiaries.
- Keeping the only copy somewhere nobody can find it.

## Alongside a will

Think about a Lasting Power of Attorney at the same time. A will deals with things after death; an LPA covers decisions while someone is alive but unable to make them. See our LPA guide.
