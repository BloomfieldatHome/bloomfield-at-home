Bathroom aids for the bath, shower and toilet - a plain-English guide

Updated 23 November 2026 · General information, not professional advice

The bathroom is often the first room where things start to feel harder. Getting in and out of the bath, standing in the shower or getting up from the toilet can become tiring or a bit wobbly long before anything else in the house does. The good news is that most of the answers are simple, inexpensive and quick to fit.

This guide explains the common bathroom aids, what each one is for, and what to check before buying. But start with the free route.

Ask for the free assessment first

In England, anyone who is finding daily tasks difficult can ask their council's adult social care team for a needs assessment. This often includes an occupational therapist (OT), who looks at how the person actually uses the bathroom and suggests the right equipment and where it should go.

This matters for two reasons:

  • The right aid in the right place. An OT can spot that a bath board will not suit the shape of your bath, or that a grab rail needs to be on the other side of the toilet. Buying the wrong thing is a common and frustrating mistake.
  • It may cost nothing. In England, councils cannot charge for aids, or for minor adaptations costing £1,000 or less, when they are provided to meet assessed needs (Care and Support Regulations 2014, checked 23 November 2026). That often covers grab rails, raised toilet seats and bath boards.

Waiting lists for an OT visit vary a lot between areas. If the wait is long, it is reasonable to buy simple, low-cost items in the meantime, but keep the assessment request in. In Wales and Scotland, contact the local council; in Northern Ireland, contact your Health and Social Care Trust.

Getting in and out of the bath

Bath boards sit across the top of the bath. The person sits on the board, swings their legs over, and washes sitting down, often with a shower attachment. Measure the inside width of the bath at the point where the board will sit, and check the board's weight limit.

Bath seats hang a little lower inside the bath, so the person sits in some water. They need more leg strength and are not right for everyone.

Bath lifts are battery-powered seats that lower the person into the water and raise them back up. They are more expensive and are worth discussing with an OT first.

Non-slip mats go inside the bath or shower tray, with a second non-slip mat for stepping out onto. Replace suction mats when the cups stop gripping.

Sitting in the shower

Shower stools and chairs let someone sit while they wash, which helps if standing for long is tiring. A chair with a back and arms gives more support than a stool. Check:

  • the legs are height-adjustable and have rubber feet;
  • it fits in the shower tray with room for feet;
  • the weight limit suits the person.

Long-handled sponges and toe washers help with washing the back, legs and feet without bending or twisting.

The toilet

Raised toilet seats lift the seat by around 5cm to 15cm, so there is less distance to sit down and stand up. Some clip on, some have brackets. Check the shape of the toilet pan (round or oval) and choose a height where the person's feet are flat on the floor and knees are roughly level with hips when sitting.

Toilet frames stand around the toilet and give arm rests to push up from. Measure the width around the toilet and check the frame's height adjusts. Some raised seats come with built-in arms.

Grab rails

Grab rails beside the toilet, bath and shower give something solid to hold. Fixed rails must be screwed into a solid wall or properly supported with the right fixings. If in doubt, ask a handyperson service or a qualified fitter. Suction rails are sold widely, but they can come loose without warning and should not be relied on to take someone's weight.

If you are 60 or over and pay a business to supply and install grab rails, a bath lift or a built-in shower seat in your own home, the work can be charged at the reduced 5% VAT rate. You will usually be asked to sign a short declaration (HMRC VAT Notice 701/7, checked 23 November 2026). Separately, people with a long-term illness or disability may be able to buy some equipment designed for disabled people, such as toilet frames, without VAT. Ask the supplier.

When bigger changes are needed

If the bath itself has become the problem, the usual answer is a level-access or walk-in shower. In England this can be funded by a Disabled Facilities Grant from the council, depending on income and savings. The grant is currently up to £30,000 in England, and the government has said it will review that limit (checked 23 November 2026). An OT assessment is normally the first step. Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own schemes.

Before you buy

  • Measure first: bath width, toilet height, shower tray size and the space around the toilet.
  • Check weight limits on anything someone will sit on or lean on.
  • Try equipment out with the person if you can, and ask them what feels comfortable.
  • If someone has had a fall or is struggling more than before, tell their GP. Many areas have a falls prevention service, and a physiotherapist or OT can help.
