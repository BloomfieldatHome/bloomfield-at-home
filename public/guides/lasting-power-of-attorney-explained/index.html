Lasting Power of Attorney explained - and how to do it yourself

Updated 21 November 2026 · General information, not professional advice

A Lasting Power of Attorney (LPA) is a legal document that lets someone choose people they trust (their "attorneys") to make decisions for them. It is one of the most useful things a family can sort out early, because once someone has lost the mental capacity to make decisions, it is too late to make one.

This guide covers England and Wales. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own systems.

The two types

  • Property and financial affairs: paying bills, managing bank accounts, dealing with pensions, selling a house. This one can be used while the person still has capacity, if they agree.
  • Health and welfare: decisions about care, medical treatment and where someone lives. This one can only be used once the person can no longer make those decisions themselves.

Many people make both.

What happens without one

If someone loses capacity without an LPA, the family may need to apply to the Court of Protection to be appointed as a deputy. That is slower, more expensive and involves ongoing supervision.

Doing it yourself - the official route

You do not need a solicitor. The government's online service guides you through each step and checks for common mistakes. Search "make a lasting power of attorney" on gov.uk.

  • The fee to register each LPA is £92 (since November 2025), so £184 for both types.
  • People on a low income may pay half, or nothing - check the fee reduction rules on gov.uk.
  • The forms must be signed by the person making it, the attorneys, and a "certificate provider" who confirms the person understands what they are signing.
  • Registration with the Office of the Public Guardian takes several weeks, so don't leave it until the last minute.

When paying for help makes sense

Paid services - from online LPA services to solicitors - can help if the family situation is complicated, there are concerns about pressure from relatives, or you simply want someone to check everything. The registration fee is paid on top.

Practical tips

  • Choose attorneys who are organised, trustworthy and likely to be around for years.
  • Think about whether attorneys must act together or can act separately - it affects how easy things are later.
  • Once registered, tell the bank and GP, and keep copies somewhere safe.
